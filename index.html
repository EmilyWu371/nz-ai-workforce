AI and New Zealand’s Workforce: What The Data Shows

~6 minute read Sources: Stats NZ, Anthropic, BCG, Deloitte Access Economics / IMD
So what: five moves for NZ leadership teams
  1. Don’t mistake usage for value. New Zealand already ranks near the top of the world on AI usage/adoption but what actually matters is whether a specific, measurable business outcome has moved. Internal reporting should be wary of overindexing on just adoption metrics (seats licensed, queries run, pilots launched).
  2. Pick one high-value workflow and redesign it end to end rather than spreading AI thinly. Choose one with a clear before/after financial metric, and rebuild the steps, decision rights, and accountability around it.
  3. Fund training and safeguards inside the redesign, not as a separate HR programme. With only a quarter of the workforce trained, a redesign that skips capability-building is likely to underperform its own business case.
  4. Treat this as a leadership commitment, not an IT project. Companies pulling ahead share multi-year efforts sponsored directly by the CEO, not pilots handed to a single team.
  5. Be willing to learn from others’ experiences. High national usage can feel like being ahead but in reality, few organisations globally have fully solved the redesign problem. There’s value in Kiwi businesses watching and learning from what others are doing.

AI task exposure

  • Mapping OpenAI’s occupational exposure research (Eloundou et al., 2024) onto Stats NZ’s 2023 Census puts economy-wide AI task exposure at roughly 35%, weighted by employment.
  • This describes how much of the economy’s work AI could plausibly help with; it is not a measure of how many jobs will disappear.
  • Most exposed sectors: Financial & Insurance Services (48.9%), Information Media & Telecommunications (46.9%), Professional, Scientific & Technical Services (46.5%), Public Administration & Safety (41.9%).

AI usage

  • On Anthropic’s Usage Index, New Zealand ranks 5th of 121 countries measured, at 4.84x expected usage (expected = 1.0x, based on population share).
  • Use split: Work 44.4% / Personal 41.0% / Coursework 14.6%.
  • Modest lean towards collaborative use over full delegation (human stays in the loop).
  • Usage concentrated in Computer & Mathematical (22.7%), Arts, Design, Entertainment, Sports & Media (14.9%), and Educational Instruction & Library (12.8%), which together account for over half of all NZ usage.
  • Most frequent topic is homework help, at 8.8% of conversations. The next several are writing-related: self-presentation, promotional, and workplace writing.

Productivity growth

  • Labour productivity growth: ~1.75%/yr (1996–2008) → ~0.70%/yr (2008–2024) → ~0.20%/yr (5 yrs to March 2024).
  • Latest year (to March 2025): labour productivity +0.8%, but multifactor productivity (output growth net of added capital and labour) fell 0.9%.
  • NZ ranks 63rd of 67 countries on IMD’s World Competitiveness measure for productivity and efficiency (Deloitte Access Economics, 2025, citing IMD 2024).

Why access and usage haven’t shown up in the numbers

  • Access and usage signal that NZ workers are taking AI seriously, but it doesn’t translate to economic value.
  • BCG’s Build for the Future 2025 study
    • This study sorts 1,250 companies worldwide into four stages: 5% are “future-built” and generating real revenue or cash-flow gains. 35% are “scaling” and starting to see value. The remaining 60% are “laggards,” often using AI widely with little financial return to show for it.
    • Future-built companies significantly outperform: 1.7x the revenue growth, 3.6x the three-year shareholder return, and 1.6x the EBIT margin of laggards.
  • A separate BCG survey, AI at Work 2025
    • 72% of companies have rolled out AI tools, but only 50% have started redesigning workflows around them, and just 22% have used AI to build new products or revenue streams.
    • The companies that do move beyond tool rollout invest with intent: 80% of their AI budget goes into redesigning and inventing, concentrated in a handful of core processes, rather than spread thinly across many.
  • The pattern across both studies is consistent: most companies surveyed are currently giving employees access to AI, but far fewer go on to redesign how the work actually gets done. That’s where the value shows up.
  • New Zealand’s high usage and exposure describe access. Nothing in the data suggests NZ companies have gone further than that on average.

The workforce is willing, but under-prepared

  • BCG’s research on future-built companies consistently finds upskilling and workflow redesign moving together: redesign a process without training the people who run it, and it tends not to get adopted.
  • Only ~25% of New Zealanders have received any AI training; this isn’t unique to New Zealand. BCG’s global AI at Work survey (10,635 employees, 11 countries) found only 36% of employees feel properly trained.

What this means for NZ executives

1
Don’t mistake usage as a proxy for value.If internal reporting leans on adoption metrics (seats licensed, queries run, pilots launched), it’s tracking something already high in NZ. What matters is whether a specific, measurable outcome tied to organisational value has moved.
2
Pick one high-value workflow and redesign it end to end.Rather than spreading AI thinly across many processes, choose one with clear before/after financial metrics and rebuild the steps, decision rights, and accountability around AI from the start.
3
Fund training and safeguards as part of the same initiative.Not a separate HR programme. With only a quarter of the workforce trained, a redesign that skips capability-building is likely to underperform its own business case.
4
Treat this as a leadership commitment, not an IT project.Future-built companies share multi-year efforts sponsored directly by the CEO, not pilots handed to IT or a single business unit, because the redesign work needs training, workflow, and accountability changes across the whole business.
5
Be willing to learn from others.High usage and exposure can create a sense of being ahead, but few organisations anywhere, in NZ or globally, have cracked this yet. There’s value in studying how other companies and markets are approaching the redesign work.
